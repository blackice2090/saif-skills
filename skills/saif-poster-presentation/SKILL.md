# Scientific Poster Presentation Skills: From Preparation to Impactful Presence

## Purpose

Help a contestant turn a scientific poster into a clear, evidence-based conversation with visitors and judges. Focus on the spoken explanation, audience interaction, delivery, and rehearsal. Do not rewrite or redesign the poster here; route that work to `saif-scientific-poster`.

## Source classification

Label claims and recommendations using these categories:

- **OFFICIAL REQUIREMENT / OFFICIAL GUIDANCE** — a current requirement or instruction stated by SAIF's official materials. Link or name the source and recheck it before advising on a live competition. The latest official update supplied on 2026-11-07 gives six minutes to present and two minutes for judge questions.
- **EXPERT GUIDANCE** — presentation and communication advice summarized in `references/poster_presentation_notes.md` from the supplied training session.
- **TRAINING EXAMPLE** — hypothetical values, scenarios, scripts, or time allocations used to teach a technique. Clearly mark them as examples and never present them as a participant's results or official rules.
- **PRACTICAL COACHING** — an internal exercise, checklist, rehearsal plan, or readiness judgment. It is advisory, not an official SAIF score or criterion.

If an official requirement is unknown or sources conflict, say so and direct the user to the current SAIF participant page or guide. Never infer a rule from a training video.

## Core principle

A strong poster presentation combines:

1. A clear scientific message supported by evidence.
2. A poster that helps a viewer find the contribution and result quickly.
3. A presenter who explains what the evidence means, acknowledges its limits, and invites a useful conversation.

The poster is a visual anchor, not a script to read aloud.

## Workflow

### 1. Gather the presentation facts

Ask for or reuse what is known about the problem, contribution, method, prototype, data, metric, comparison, result, limitations, next step, poster, team roles, audience, and current official presentation instructions. Mark missing facts as unknown. Do not invent results, project ownership, or competition rules.

### 2. Check the central claim

For each headline result, identify what was tested, on which data, using what metric, against what comparison, and what the result does not establish. Keep the spoken explanation consistent with the poster, submitted project information, and supporting files.

Use precise language. For example, a result on a test dataset does not by itself prove real-world impact or protection. Distinguish percentage points from relative percentage change. Flag words such as “advanced,” “best,” “guaranteed,” or “fully secure” unless defined and supported.

### 3. Build a concise opening

Draft a natural introduction that answers, in order:

1. What problem does the project address?
2. Why is the gap worth addressing?
3. What did the team build or investigate?
4. What did the evidence show?
5. What is the next step or key limitation?

Keep it short enough to open a conversation. Do not force a visitor through the entire poster before learning what they want to know. Offer a choice such as whether they would like to hear about the method, result, or application.

### 4. Guide the poster walkthrough

Use the viewer's attention as a path:

- **From a distance:** make the topic and reason to stop apparent.
- **After approaching:** point out the contribution and main result without requiring a long read.
- **During discussion:** use the method, data source, metric, comparison, and limitations to explain how much confidence the evidence supports.

Follow a coherent story: problem → contribution → method → evidence and result → limitation → next step. Explain the figure or number the visitor is looking at; avoid reciting blocks of text.

### 5. Adapt to the listener

Use the visitor's questions to find the right level of detail. A conversation may focus on technical choices and evaluation, intended users and adoption, or expected impact and how it would be measured. Do not infer a visitor's interests from appearance or title. Keep the underlying facts and limits consistent in every version.

### 6. Use the poster and body language well

Recommended **EXPERT GUIDANCE**:

- Stand beside the poster so important content stays visible.
- Point to the relevant chart, diagram, or section while explaining it.
- Keep natural eye contact and notice whether the listener is following.
- Welcome visitors with a brief opening, then pause and listen.
- Avoid reading the poster, blocking results, pointing without explanation, focusing on a phone, or continuing a long monologue after the visitor's question is answered.

### 7. Coordinate a team

Agree who leads each part and how to hand off naturally. Make sure each member can explain the whole project and their own contribution. Rotate roles when useful during a long exhibition, and avoid interrupting or contradicting one another in front of visitors.

### 8. Plan timing from current official guidance

Check the current SAIF participant page or guide each time a real presentation is planned. The latest official SAIF timing update supplied on 2026-11-07 states **6 minutes to present the project, followed by 2 minutes for judge questions**. Use this as the current official timing, while rechecking the live guidance before a real session because instructions can change.

Create a timed outline that fits the six-minute presentation and rehearse to leave room for natural transitions. Label any suggested allocation within the six minutes as **PRACTICAL COACHING**, not official judging weights. Do not use the older training-session figures of seven minutes for the presentation and ten minutes for questions as current official timing.

### 9. Rehearse and refine

Practice aloud, using the actual poster or a view of it. Rehearse the opening, transitions, explanation of the strongest figure, interpretation of the result, likely follow-up questions, team handoffs, and closing. Ask someone unfamiliar with the project:

- What problem is this project trying to solve?
- What did the team contribute or find?
- What evidence do you remember?
- What remains unclear?

Use their answers to improve clarity. Check that the spoken numbers match the source data and poster, the figures are readable in the actual display setting, and any QR links work.

## Handling questions

For ordinary presentation questions, use:

**Direct answer → evidence or measurement → relevant limitation → next step, if useful.**

If a test has not been run, say so plainly and distinguish completed work from planned work. Route requests for adversarial judge questioning, claim-by-claim red-teaming, or extensive defense preparation to `saif-jury-readiness`.

## Output format

When coaching a contestant, provide only the sections useful for the request:

### Readiness snapshot

Use Green / Amber / Red as **PRACTICAL COACHING**, never as an official score.

### Core message

One evidence-backed sentence.

### Opening and walkthrough

A natural spoken opening and a sequence tied to the actual poster.

### Evidence and limits to explain

The key result, its basis, and any limitation that changes its interpretation.

### Delivery and team notes

Specific suggestions for posture, audience interaction, transitions, or role handoffs.

### Rehearsal task

One focused practice exercise and a clear completion check.

## Escalation and boundaries

- Use `saif-scientific-poster` for poster content, section order, layout, graphics, or template compliance.
- Use `saif-jury-readiness` for rigorous judge-question preparation and adversarial review of claims.
- Use `saif-submission-compliance` for required files and submission rules.
- Use `saif-orchestrator` when the user needs help choosing the next competition stage.
